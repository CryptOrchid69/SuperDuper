Complete Guide to Modems and Routers: How They Work & Everything About Latency

PART 1: WHAT IS A MODEM? (Simple Explanation)
The Basic Idea
Think of a modem as a translator or interpreter. Just like an interpreter translates English to Spanish and Spanish back to English, 
a modem translates information between two different "languages" that computers use.

The Word "Modem":

Modem = Modulator + Demodulator
Modulator = Converts digital signals to analog signals
Demodulator = Converts analog signals back to digital signals
What Does a Modem Do?
Coming INTO Your Home (Download):

Your Internet Service Provider (ISP) sends signals through a cable, telephone line, or fiber optic cable to your house
These signals come in as analog signals (for cable/DSL) or light pulses (for fiber)
Your devices (computers, phones, tablets) speak in digital code (1s and 0s, also called binary)
The modem demodulates (converts) those analog/light signals into digital signals your devices can understand
Now your device can read the information!
Going OUT of Your Home (Upload):

You click something on your computer - it creates digital data (1s and 0s)
The modem modulates (converts) that digital data into analog signals or light pulses
These signals travel through the cable/telephone line/fiber to the ISP
The ISP receives and processes your request
Real-World Analogy

Imagine you have a friend who speaks only Spanish, and you speak only English. A translator (modem) sits between you:

Your friend speaks Spanish → Translator converts to English for you
You speak English → Translator converts to Spanish for your friend
Types of Modems



Type	Connection	Speed	Details
Cable Modem	Coaxial cable (like cable TV)	Fast	Most common in homes
DSL Modem	Telephone lines	Slower	Works over phone lines
Fiber-Optic Modem	Fiber optic cables	Very Fast	Uses light signals, fastest option
Satellite Modem	Satellite dishes	Medium	For remote areas
Important Note About Modems
Your modem connects directly to your ISP
It gives your entire home network one public IP address (like your home's address on the internet)
Without a modem, you have NO internet connection - period
Modern ISPs often combine modem + router into one device (called a "gateway")

PART 2: WHAT IS A ROUTER? (Simple Explanation)
The Basic Idea
Think of a router as a traffic director, receptionist, or distribution hub. It takes the internet connection from the modem and distributes 
it to all your devices in your home.

What Does a Router Do?
Main Functions:

Takes the internet from the modem - It receives the internet connection through an Ethernet cable
Creates a local network - It creates your home WiFi network and assigns each device its own private IP address
Directs data traffic - It reads where data packets need to go and sends them to the right device
Provides WiFi - It broadcasts a wireless signal so devices can connect without cables
Provides security - It includes a firewall to protect your network from unwanted access
Manages connections - It can handle multiple devices connecting at the same time
Real-World Analogy #1: The Receptionist
Modem = The main telephone line coming into a business
Router = The receptionist who answers calls and transfers them to the right extension
Your devices = Different extensions (your phone, computer, printer, etc.)
Real-World Analogy #2: The Highway Entrance
Modem = The entrance ramp to a highway bringing traffic in
Router = The local roads that distribute that traffic to different houses in a neighborhood
What Routers Actually Do (Technical):
A router looks at data packets (small packages of information) and reads the destination IP address on each packet. It then consults its 
routing table (a list of where to send different types of traffic) and forwards the packet to the correct device.

Example: When you download a video from Netflix:

Netflix sends data packets to your router's IP address
Your router reads: "This packet is for device #192.168.1.5" (your TV)
Your router sends it to your TV
Your TV receives the video
Types of Routers



Type	Details
Wireless Router	Has WiFi antennas, broadcasts signal through the air
Wired Router	Uses only Ethernet cables, no WiFi
Mesh Router	Multiple units working together to cover a large home
Edge Router/Gateway	Connects your home network to the ISP (often combined with modem)
Important Note About Routers
Routers can work WITHOUT internet (you can still share files between devices)
They are optional if you only have one device and use a wired connection
They're essential if you have multiple devices or want WiFi
Modern routers have built-in security features

PART 3: HOW MODEMS AND ROUTERS WORK TOGETHER
The Connection Flow


[ISP Internet Signal]
        ↓
    [MODEM]
(Converts ISP signal to digital)
        ↓
[ROUTER]
(Distributes internet to all devices)
        ↓
    [YOUR DEVICES]
(Laptop, Phone, TV, Printer, etc.)
Step-by-Step Process
When you want to browse the internet:

Modem receives ISP signal

The ISP sends a signal through the cable/fiber to your modem
Modem demodulates (converts) it to digital format
Router receives from modem

Router is connected to modem via Ethernet cable
Router receives the digital signal
Router broadcasts WiFi

Router creates a WiFi network (like "Linksys" or "MyNetwork")
Devices connect to this WiFi
Router directs traffic

When you request a website, your device sends a request
Router reads the request and sends it to the ISP through the modem
ISP finds the website and sends data back
Router receives data and sends it to your specific device
Device receives information

Your device gets the data and displays the website
Key Difference: LAN vs WAN
LAN (Local Area Network) = Your home network, handled by the router

All your home devices talking to each other
Private IP addresses (like 192.168.1.5)
WAN (Wide Area Network) = The internet, handled by the modem

Connection to the outside world through your ISP
Public IP address (your home's internet address)
Do You Need Both?



Scenario	Need Modem?	Need Router?
Single device, wired only	YES	NO
Multiple devices, WiFi	YES	YES
Local file sharing only	NO	YES
Modern home setup	YES (often combined)	YES (often combined)

PART 4: UNDERSTANDING ROUTER LATENCY (Detailed)
What is Latency?
Latency = The time delay between when you send a request and when you receive a response, measured in milliseconds (ms).

Think of it like shouting across a valley:

You shout "HELLO!" (your request)
The echo comes back "HELLO!" (the response)
The time between your shout and hearing the echo = latency
What is "Ping"?
Ping is a test that measures latency. It sends a small packet of data to a server and measures how long it takes to come back.

Low ping = Fast response = Good for gaming and video calls
High ping = Slow response = Lag and delays
Latency vs Speed (IMPORTANT!)
Many people confuse these:

Internet Speed = How much data can be downloaded per second (measured in Mbps)

Example: "I have 100 Mbps internet"
This is like the width of a highway
Latency = How long it takes for data to reach you (measured in milliseconds)

Example: "My ping is 20ms"
This is like how fast cars travel on the highway
You can have fast internet but high latency! For example:

You download at 500 Mbps (very fast)
But each request takes 150ms to get a response (very slow)
This causes lag in games even though your internet is "fast"
Good vs Bad Latency Numbers



Latency	Quality	Best For
0-20ms	Excellent	Competitive gaming, real-time trading
20-50ms	Very Good	Online gaming, video calls
50-100ms	Acceptable	General browsing, streaming
100-150ms	Poor	Noticeable lag in gaming
150ms+	Very Poor	Unplayable games, choppy video calls

PART 5: WHAT CAUSES ROUTER LATENCY?
1. Distance (Geographical)
The Problem:

Data travels at the speed of light through cables
But longer distances = longer travel time
Every mile adds a tiny bit of delay
Example:

Request to a server 100 miles away = ~5-10ms latency
Request to a server 2,200 miles away = ~40-50ms latency
You can't really fix this, but you can:

Use servers closer to you
Use a CDN (Content Delivery Network) that caches content nearby
2. Router Processing Time
The Problem:

Your router must read each data packet
It checks where the packet should go (routing table)
This takes time, especially if the router is old or slow
What causes this:

Old/weak hardware - Outdated routers process data slowly
Overloaded router - Too many devices connected
Buggy firmware - Old software has bugs that cause delays
Complex routing - Some packets need multiple hops through different routers
How much latency?

Modern routers add 0.5-2ms per hop
Old routers might add 5-10ms
3. WiFi vs Wired Connection
WiFi Latency Issues:

WiFi is wireless - data travels through the air
The router can only talk to one device at a time
All devices must take turns = airtime sharing
This creates delays, called jitter (inconsistent latency)
Example:

Wired (Ethernet): 10ms ping
WiFi (same router): 15-20ms ping
Difference: 5-10ms added by WiFi
WiFi problems get worse when:

Many devices are connected
You're far from the router
There's interference (microwaves, other WiFi networks, cordless phones)
Wired Connection Advantages:

Direct, dedicated connection
No interference
Consistent, lower latency
10-20ms better than WiFi
4. Network Congestion
The Problem:

If too much data is flowing through your network, it gets backed up
Think of it like a traffic jam on a highway
What causes this:

Someone streaming 4K video
Someone downloading large files
Multiple devices using internet simultaneously
Router's buffer (temporary storage) gets full
Latency spike example:

Normal: 20ms ping
During congestion: 100-200ms ping
This is temporary lag that makes games stutter
5. ISP and Routing Path
The Problem:

Your ISP chooses the path your data takes to reach servers
Sometimes they don't choose the most direct route
They choose based on cost or network policy
Example:

Direct route to server: 30ms
ISP's chosen route: 80ms
You can't control this, but it affects your latency
Multi-hop problem:

Each router your data passes through adds a small delay
More hops = more delay
A request might pass through 10-15 routers before reaching the destination

6. DNS Lookup Time
What is DNS?

DNS = Domain Name System
It translates website names (google.com) into IP addresses (142.251.32.46)
This happens before your data is sent
The Problem:

Your ISP's DNS servers are often slow
Each DNS lookup adds 20-100ms to the first connection
Example:

Visiting google.com for the first time: 50ms (includes DNS lookup)
Visiting it again: 20ms (already cached)

7. Packet Loss
The Problem:

Sometimes data packets get lost during transmission
The device has to resend them
This causes delays
What causes packet loss:

Poor WiFi signal
Damaged cables
Network congestion
Interference

8. Your Device
The Problem:

Your laptop, phone, or gaming console might be slow
Running too many background apps
Old hardware
This isn't really router latency, but it affects overall responsiveness

PART 6: HOW TO REDUCE ROUTER LATENCY
Quick Fixes (Do These First - Takes 5 Minutes)

1. Power Cycle Your Router and Modem

Unplug modem from power
Wait 60 seconds
Plug modem back in
Wait for it to fully restart (lights stabilize)
Unplug router
Wait 30 seconds
Plug router back in
Wait for restart
Why it works:

Clears the router's memory cache
Resets temporary glitches
Fixes latency spikes caused by memory leaks
Effectiveness: Fixes the problem ~40% of the time

2. Switch to Wired Ethernet Connection

Use a Cat 6 Ethernet cable
Plug directly into router
Reduces latency by 10-20ms compared to WiFi
Why it works:

No WiFi interference
No airtime sharing delay
Direct, dedicated connection
When to do this:

Gaming (most important)
Video conferencing
Online trading
Any latency-sensitive activity

3. Move Closer to Your Router

WiFi signal strength affects latency
Closer = stronger signal = lower latency
Move within 10-20 feet of router if possible

4. Close Background Applications

Streaming services
Downloads
Video apps
File syncing (Dropbox, OneDrive)
Why it works:

Reduces network congestion
Frees up router processing power
Medium-Level Fixes (Takes 15-30 Minutes)

5. Change Your DNS Servers

Your ISP's DNS is often slow
Switch to faster DNS servers:
Google DNS: 8.8.8.8 and 8.8.4.4
Cloudflare DNS: 1.1.1.1 and 1.0.0.1
How to change (in router settings):

Open browser, go to 192.168.1.1 (or 192.168.0.1)
Log in to router
Find "DNS Settings"
Enter new DNS addresses
Save and restart
Expected improvement: 5-20ms reduction on first page loads

6. Update Router Firmware

Router software gets updates
Updates fix bugs and improve performance
How:

Log into router settings (192.168.1.1)
Look for "System" or "Administration"
Find "Firmware Update"
Check for updates and install
Why it matters:

Fixes latency bugs
Improves packet processing
Enhances security

7. Enable Quality of Service (QoS)

QoS = Quality of Service
Prioritizes certain traffic (like gaming) over other traffic (like streaming)
How to enable:

Log into router settings
Find "QoS" or "Traffic Management"
Set gaming device as high priority
Set streaming devices as low priority
Result: Gaming traffic gets priority, reducing latency during streaming

8. Reduce Number of Connected Devices

Each device on WiFi adds overhead
Disconnect devices you're not using
Move non-essential devices to 2.4GHz band if available
Why it helps:

Router has less work to do
More bandwidth for your device
Lower latency
Advanced Fixes (Requires Technical Knowledge or Hardware Purchase)

9. Upgrade Your Router

Modern routers process data faster
WiFi 6 (802.11ax) and WiFi 7 (802.11be) are much better
Reduces latency by 5-30ms compared to older routers
What to look for:

WiFi 6 or WiFi 7
Multiple antennas
Good reviews for low latency
Dual-band or tri-band support

10. Use a Mesh WiFi System

Multiple routers working together
Better coverage and more consistent latency
Devices automatically connect to the strongest router
Brands:

TP-Link Archer
Netgear Orbi
Eero
Google Nest WiFi
Latency improvement: 5-15ms reduction in coverage areas

11. Use a Wired Connection Alternative (If You Can't Run Cables)

Option A: MoCA Adapter

Uses your coaxial cable (cable TV wiring)
Creates wired connection quality over existing cables
Cost: $30-50 per pair
Latency: Same as Ethernet (very low)
Option B: Powerline Adapter

Uses electrical wiring in your home
Slower than MoCA but better than WiFi
Cost: $20-40 per pair
Latency: 5-10ms better than WiFi
12. Use a CDN (Content Delivery Network)

Services like Cloudflare, Akamai, or Fastly
Caches content closer to you
Reduces distance data must travel
This is more for websites/services, not something you directly control

13. Switch to Fiber Internet (If Available)

Fiber is fundamentally faster and lower latency
Uses light signals instead of copper wire
Typical latency: 5-15ms (vs 20-40ms for cable)
Cost: Usually more expensive

14. Upgrade Your ISP

Some ISPs have better routing paths
Some use newer infrastructure
Fiber ISPs generally have lower latency

PART 7: ROUTER LATENCY TESTING
How to Measure Your Latency
Online Ping Test:

Go to speedtest.net
Click "Go" button
Look for "Ping" (measured in ms)
Alternative sites:

pingtest.net
speedof.me
fast.com (shows basic ping)
What to Look For
Good test results:

Run test 3-4 times
Take the average
Test at different times of day
Test on both WiFi and wired if possible
Compare your results:

WiFi ping vs Wired ping
Morning vs evening (to see congestion effects)
Before and after making changes
Gaming Ping Test
For gaming, you want to test ping to game servers, not just internet servers:

How:

Most games show ping in-game (look at settings)
Look for "ping," "latency," or "ms" in game stats
Watch how it changes during gameplay

PART 8: SPECIAL SITUATIONS
Gaming Optimization
Priority order for low latency:

Use wired Ethernet connection (most important)
Upgrade to modern WiFi 6/7 router
Enable QoS in router (prioritize gaming)
Close background apps and streaming
Use gaming-optimized router settings
Consider fiber internet if available
Target ping for different games:

Competitive FPS (CS:GO, Valorant): 0-30ms
Fighting games: 0-50ms
MOBA (League of Legends, DOTA 2): 0-100ms
RPGs and casual games: 0-150ms
Video Conferencing (Zoom, Teams, Skype)
What matters:

Latency is less critical than bandwidth
But high latency causes:
Echo delays
Delayed responses
Awkward pauses
Optimization:

Use wired connection if possible
Close other apps using bandwidth
Enable QoS for video conferencing
Use WiFi 6 router if on WiFi
Target: 50-100ms is fine, but lower is better

Video Streaming (Netflix, YouTube, Twitch)
What matters:

Bandwidth is more important than latency
Latency matters only for live streaming (Twitch)
For watching:

Any latency under 100ms is fine
Bandwidth matters more
For live streaming:

Low latency streaming needs 5-20ms
Requires specialized setup
Home Office/Remote Work
What causes issues:

High latency during video calls
Slow response times when uploading files
Lag when using cloud applications
Optimization:

Use wired connection for critical work
Enable QoS for work applications
Avoid downloading/streaming during important calls
Use modern WiFi 6 router
